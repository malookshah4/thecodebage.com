On a short video feed, people decide very fast whether to keep watching. Their thumb is already moving. If the first few seconds don't give them a reason to stay, they're gone, and the rest of your video (the part you spent the most time on) never gets seen.

That's why creators obsess over the hook. I don't think the hook is everything. A good opening can't save a boring video. But a weak opening can easily bury a good one. This post is about the practical side: what to put in those first seconds, what to cut, and how to tell from your analytics whether your hook is the problem.

## Start with the payoff or the problem

The most common hook mistake is building up to the point. In a long YouTube video you have some room for setup. In a short video you don't.

Two openings that work well:

**Lead with the payoff.** Show the result first. The finished cake, the clean room, the before-and-after, the trick landing. Then show how you got there. People stay to see the process because they've already seen it's worth it.

**Lead with the problem.** Name something the viewer is struggling with. "Your phone photos look blurry at night, and it's probably not the camera." Now they want the answer.

Compare these two openings for the same video:

- "Hey guys, so today I wanted to show you a quick tip I found for cooking rice..."
- "If your rice always comes out sticky, you're skipping one step."

The second one says who it's for and promises something in under three seconds. The first one hasn't said anything yet.

## Cut the intro and the greeting

"Hi everyone, welcome back to my channel." Every second of that is a second where the viewer has nothing to hold on to. Logos, intro music, "before we start, follow me", all of it belongs at the end or nowhere.

A useful editing habit: record normally, then in the edit, start the video at the first sentence that actually says something. Very often that's your second or third sentence. Just cut everything before it. It feels abrupt the first time you do it. Viewers don't notice. They just notice when it's slow.

## Put movement in the very first frame

The first frame is basically a still image the viewer sees while the video starts. If it's you sitting still about to talk, or a static product on a table, there's nothing pulling the eye.

Some ways to fix that:

- Start mid-action. Hands already chopping, paint already moving, you already walking toward the camera.
- Start with a quick zoom or a cut on the first beat.
- Have something enter the frame right away.
- If you're talking to camera, start mid-sentence with energy instead of taking a breath first.

You don't need crazy effects. Just make sure frame one isn't a dead frame.

## Add an on-screen text hook

A lot of people watch with the sound off (I wrote more about that in [why captions matter on short videos](/blog/why-captions-matter-on-short-videos/)). If your hook is only spoken, those viewers miss it. Put a short line of text on screen in the first second.

Good text hooks are short enough to read in one glance. Around five to eight words is a decent target. "3 settings ruining your photos." "I tested the cheapest one." "Don't buy this until you see this." The text can match what you say, or it can add context your voice doesn't, like "Day 1 of learning guitar at 40".

Keep it away from the bottom of the screen where the app's own buttons and description sit.

## Open loops, carefully

An open loop is a question your video raises early and answers later. "The third one surprised me." "Wait until you see what happens when I add water." It works because people like closure. They'll stay to get the answer.

Two rules for open loops:

1. Actually close the loop. If you promise a surprise, deliver it.
2. Don't put the payoff at the very end of a long video if the middle is weak. People will skip or leave. Sometimes it's better to close one small loop early and open another.

## Don't clickbait

It's tempting to promise something huge in the hook and then deliver something ordinary. It might even get people to stay for a few seconds. But when viewers feel tricked, they leave quickly, they don't like or share, and some of them leave annoyed comments. Platforms pay attention to how long people watch and how they react, not just whether they started watching. A hook that overpromises usually shows up as a sharp drop right after the moment people realise there's no real payoff.

There's a longer-term cost too. Viewers remember. If your last three videos didn't deliver, people scroll past the fourth one.

The honest version of a strong hook is a true promise, said in an interesting way.

## Match the hook to the thumbnail and caption

On platforms that show a cover image or let people see your caption before they tap, the hook needs to continue what those promised. If the cover says "Easy 5-minute pasta" and the video starts with you unpacking groceries for 20 seconds, there's a mismatch and people leave.

Think of the cover, the caption and the first three seconds as one message. The cover makes a promise, the caption adds a detail, and the first seconds confirm "yes, this is the thing you tapped for".

## How to read a retention graph

Most platforms give you some version of an audience retention graph in your analytics. It shows what percentage of viewers are still watching at each point in the video. Learning to read it is the single most useful skill for fixing hooks.

What to look for:

- **A steep drop in the first 0 to 3 seconds.** Some drop is normal on every video. A very steep one means the opening isn't grabbing people. This is a hook problem.
- **A gentle, steady decline.** Normal. People drift off over time.
- **A sudden dip in the middle.** Something specific happened there. A slow section, a confusing cut, a moment where the video seemed to end, or the point where you finally revealed the answer and people had what they came for.
- **Bumps going up.** People rewatching a part. Often a sign of something interesting, or something that went by too fast to follow.

Compare graphs across several of your own videos, not against other creators. Your own videos are the fairest comparison, because the audience is the same.

## Test variations

Hooks are hard to judge in your head. What you think is clever might not land, and the line you almost cut might be the one that works.

Some ways to test:

- Post the same core video with two different openings, a few days apart, and compare the early retention.
- Try the same topic with a payoff-first hook one time and a problem-first hook another time.
- Change only one thing at a time where you can. If you change the hook, the length and the music all together, you won't know which one mattered.

Results on any single video are noisy, so don't read too much into one post. Look for patterns over five or ten tries.
