Reaction videos are everywhere, and so are the copyright claims that come with them. Some reaction channels run for years without trouble. Others get videos blocked, lose monetization, or get strikes. The difference usually isn't luck. It's mostly about how much of the original they show and how much of their own stuff they add.

Before anything else: **I'm not a lawyer.** This is general information from someone who builds creator tools and has read a lot about this, not legal advice. Fair use is a US legal idea. Other countries have their own rules (the UK and many Commonwealth countries use "fair dealing", which is narrower, and plenty of places have different exceptions entirely). If you're making money from reactions or someone threatens you with legal action, talk to an actual lawyer in your country.

With that said, here's how I understand the basics.

## What fair use actually is

In the US, fair use lets you use parts of copyrighted work without permission in some situations, like commentary, criticism, news reporting, teaching or parody. It's not a list of safe actions. It's a defense that gets decided case by case, by looking at four factors together.

In plain words, those four factors are:

1. **Purpose and character of your use.** Are you adding something new, like commentary, criticism or analysis? Or are you just showing the original again? Commercial use counts against you a bit, but it doesn't kill a fair use claim by itself.
2. **Nature of the original work.** Using factual stuff (a news clip, a tutorial) is treated more kindly than using highly creative work (a movie, a song, a music video).
3. **Amount used.** How much of the original did you take, and did you take the "heart" of it, the most important part?
4. **Effect on the market.** Does your video replace the original? If someone can watch your reaction and get the whole experience without ever watching the original, that's a problem.

No single factor wins. A court weighs all of them. That's also why nobody can give you a simple rule like "under 30 seconds is fine". People repeat that rule a lot. It's not real.

## Transformative vs face in the corner

The first factor is where most reaction channels win or lose. The key word you'll hear is "transformative", meaning your video adds new meaning, message or expression.

A transformative reaction might look like this: you pause a trailer to point out details, explain a reference, compare it to the source book, disagree with a choice, or share real background knowledge. The original is the material you're talking about. You are the main content.

The weak version is the one everybody has seen. Someone plays a full music video or a full episode, puts their face in a little box in the corner, and says "wow" and "no way" a few times. That's basically a re-upload with decoration. Courts and platforms see that as taking the original and adding almost nothing, and honestly I think viewers notice too.

There was a well-known US case involving the h3h3Productions channel (Hosseinzadeh v. Klein, 2017) where the court found their video fair use, partly because it mixed short clips with a lot of critical commentary. That's often cited as an example of the good kind. It's one case, though, decided on its own facts. It doesn't mean every reaction video is protected.

## How much of the original to show

Less is safer. That's the boring answer, and it's the true one.

Some practical habits:

- Show only the parts you're actually commenting on. If you have nothing to say about a section, skip it.
- Pause often. Every pause where you talk is time that's clearly yours.
- Don't play the most important part in full if you can avoid it, for example the full reveal of a twist or the whole chorus of a song.
- Avoid reacting to something from start to end without breaks. A full 10-minute video with your face in the corner is a full 10-minute copy.

A rough self-check I like: if you muted the original and listened only to yourself, would your video still make sense and have something to say? If it's mostly silence and gasps, you're leaning on the original too much.

## Content ID claims vs copyright strikes

On YouTube these are two different things, and people mix them up all the time.

A **Content ID claim** is automatic. The copyright owner has uploaded reference files, and YouTube's system matches parts of your video against them. When it finds a match, the owner's chosen policy kicks in. Usually that means they take the ad money from your video, or they block it in some countries, or sometimes everywhere. A claim doesn't count against your channel's standing. It just affects that video.

A **copyright strike** happens when the owner sends a formal takedown request. The video is removed and your channel gets a strike. YouTube's policy is that three active strikes can get your channel terminated. Strikes expire after a period if you complete their copyright training and don't get more, but you don't want to collect them.

You can dispute claims and counter takedowns if you really believe your use is fair. Be careful with counter-notifications, though. They are legal statements, and the other side can respond by going to court. Other platforms like TikTok and Instagram have their own systems that work somewhat differently, and they tend to just mute audio or remove the video without much explanation.

## Music is extra risky

If there's one category I'd tell people to be careful with, it's music. Music labels are among the most active rights holders when it comes to Content ID, and their matching is very good. Even a few seconds of a popular song can trigger a claim.

Reacting to a music video means the song is playing through most of your video, which is exactly what the system is built to catch. Reacting to a vlog with music in the background can also get flagged because of that background track, even if the creator you're reacting to is fine with it. Music in your own intro or outro can cause claims too.

If you want to react to music, keep clips short, talk a lot, and expect that monetization may go to the label. Many music reaction channels just accept that as part of the deal.

## Ask first, or pick creators who allow it

The simplest way to avoid most of this is permission. A lot of creators actively like reactions, because it sends viewers their way. Some say so publicly in their channel description, video description, or pinned comments. Some streamers and YouTubers have open reaction policies with conditions, like "link the original" or "don't show the whole thing".

If you plan to react to someone regularly, send a short message and ask. Keep a screenshot of the answer. Permission from the creator doesn't always cover everything (for example, the music in their video may belong to a label), but it removes the biggest risk.

Always credit and link the original in your description, even when it's not required. It's polite, and it shows good faith if there's ever a dispute.

## Layout tips that help

How you lay out the video affects how it comes across, both to viewers and, in a looser sense, to anyone judging whether you've added something.

- Give yourself real space. A tiny face bubble says the original is the main event. A split screen or a layout where you're at least half the frame says you are.
- Pause to talk instead of talking over the original. Overlapping audio is hard to follow, and the pause makes it clear the commentary is the point.
- Add your own visuals: text notes, zoom-ins on details you're explaining, a quick drawing or arrow.
- Cut the dead air. Long stretches where the original plays and you just watch are the weakest parts of any reaction.

When I built [Reel React](/apps/android/reel-react-react-quick-reel/), I added several layouts (side-by-side, top-bottom, picture-in-picture and green screen) partly for this reason. Side-by-side and top-bottom put you on equal footing with the original, which suits commentary-heavy videos. The app records your front camera next to the video, but it can't decide for you whether your use is fair. That part depends on what you say and how much you show.

If you're also thinking about how to open these videos, I wrote a separate piece on [the first three seconds of a short video](/blog/the-first-three-seconds-of-a-short-video/). For reactions, starting with your opinion instead of the original clip often works better than you'd expect.

Short version: show less, say more, credit the source, and ask when you can.
